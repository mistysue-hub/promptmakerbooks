Read, Play, Debate

How to ask Claude for nonfiction book courses on Recess: chapter modules, a game for every chapter, debates on hard questions, and practice that bends to each kid's interests.

The short version: give Claude the pages and the kids, approve a course map, test one pilot chapter, then build the rest. Everything the kids touch lives inside Recess, and nothing lands there until you say yes.

Hand Claude six things

Most of the quality is decided by the first message. These six inputs change the result more than anything else.

The chapter itself

Photos of the pages or a PDF. Questions and games get written from the actual text, not from Claude's memory of the book, and that's what keeps them accurate.

Each reader

Grade, reading level, and the habit you want to work on ("grabs the flashiest detail," "rushes"). Or ask Claude to read each kid's Recess tutor notes first.

Specific interests

"Sharks and deep-sea creatures" works. "Animals" barely does. Three to six per kid is plenty.

What "got it" means to you

Explain it to a younger sibling? Defend a position with two facts? Whatever you pick becomes the finish line for every module.

The limits

Session length, how often, topics to handle with care, and anything that's off the table.

Your approval rule

"Draft here first. Nothing goes to Recess until I say so." Recess previews every change before it's made, so this rule is easy to keep.

One focus skill per chapter

A game that tests everything tests nothing. Give each chapter one skill and rotate through them. The codes are the Common Core standards for reading informational text. The numbers stay the same from grade to grade, and only the difficulty goes up.

SkillWhat the kid is really answeringStandard
Main ideaWhat's this section mostly about, and what proves it?RI.2
Cause & effect, structureIs this a cause-and-effect, a comparison, a problem and solution, or a sequence?RI.3 · RI.5
EvidenceWhich sentence proves it?RI.1
Author's purposeWhy did the author include this? What do they want me to think?RI.6
Vocabulary in contextWhat does this word mean here, and which clue told me?RI.4
Text featuresWhat does the chart or map show that the words don't?RI.7
Weighing argumentsIs this claim backed up well enough?RI.8
Comparing sourcesWhere does another source agree or disagree?RI.9

Every third chapter is a boss round: mixed questions from all the earlier chapters, a little harder. It's spaced review in disguise, and kids will happily take on a boss.

Games where reading is the move

If a kid could win without reading the chapter, it isn't a reading game.

In "answer a question to earn a turn" games, kids learn to rush the question so they can get back to playing. In these games, playing is using the reading skill.

GameSkillHow you play
Headline DeskMain ideaPick the headline that covers the whole section. The trap choice is the juiciest single detail.
Prove ItEvidenceA claim appears. Tap the one sentence in the passage that proves it.
Chain ReactionCause & effectPut cause-and-effect cards in order. One card just happened at the same time and doesn't belong in the chain.
Guess the NumberNumbers & scaleEstimate the chapter's key figure before reading, then score by how close you came.
Word DetectiveVocabularyPick what a word means here, then tap the clue that gave it away.
Diagram DecoderText featuresAnswer questions that only the chart, map, or diagram can answer.
Claim CourtAuthor's purposeSort statements into fact, opinion, and claim that still needs proof.
Two WitnessesComparing sourcesThe book and a second source describe the same event. Flag where they disagree.
Boss RoundEverything so farMixed items from every earlier chapter, harder, with a score to beat.

How games work inside Recess

  • Each game is built as a Recess app and linked from the chapter's module. The kid never leaves Recess, and Rocky can see how it went.
  • Game moves are taps, sorts, orderings, and typed numbers. Writing and arguing happen in the conversation with Rocky, since they don't have one right answer to grade.
  • A wrong answer opens a short guided detour instead of a red X. Tell Claude which mistake you expect ("picks the most exciting detail"), and the detour will be built around it.
  • Any passage inside a game is written fresh from the chapter's facts. Nothing is copied from the book.

Debates that build thinkers, not fights

Controversial topics are where reading turns into thinking. A clear structure keeps the argument about evidence instead of about who's right.

A good debate question passes three tests

Two real sides

Thoughtful, well-informed adults actually disagree about it.

Answerable from the book

The chapter gives both sides something to work with.

About choices, not people

Debate what we should do, never who someone is.

Are zoos bad?→Should zoos keep elephants?
Are phones bad for kids?→Should phones be locked away during the school day?

The round, about 15 minutes

  1. Take a stand. Mark a spot from 0 to 10. Rocky records it.

  2. Warm up. A quick game: sort claim, evidence, and reasoning, then spot the weakest argument.

  3. Argue your assigned side with two pieces of evidence from the chapter. Rocky pushes back.

  4. Switch sides. Make the best case for the view you just argued against.

  5. Land. Mark the line again and name what moved you, even a little.

This is a format called Structured Academic Controversy, adapted for one reader and a tutor. Use that name when you ask, and Claude will know the format.

Guardrails to ask for

  • Rocky stays neutral and never gives an opinion of its own.
  • Argue with ideas, never with people. Evidence beats volume.
  • You preview any question that touches family values. Some of those belong at your kitchen table, not in the app.
  • A kid can pass on a topic, no questions asked.

How a round is scored

ClaimClear and specific · 1–3
EvidenceFrom the chapter · 1–3
ReasoningExplains why it matters · 1–3
RebuttalAnswers the other side respectfully · 1–3

Add a bonus for "I changed my mind because…" Updating your view is the skill you most want them to learn.

Two readers on the same book? Give them opposite sides and let Rocky coach each one separately. Then hold the final round at dinner, with you as the judge.

Widgets that bend to each kid

To personalize well, change the setting of the problem. The facts and the skill stay the same.

Stays the same for everyone

  • The chapter's facts
  • The focus skill
  • The right answers
  • The difficulty target

Changes for each kid

  • The scenario and the examples
  • The last question, where they use the idea in their own interest
  • How much text is on each screen
  • How many hints come before the detour

One idea, three worlds

The chapter's idea: take one species out of a food web and the effects ripple through it. The skill: follow a chain of effects.

For the ocean fan

Sea otters disappear from a kelp forest. What happens to the sea urchins, and then to the kelp?

For the wolf fan

Wolves come back to Yellowstone. What happens to the elk, and then to the willows along the rivers?

For the gamer

Every fox vanishes from your survival world. What happens to the rabbits, and then to your carrot farm?

How Recess makes this work

  • Build once, version per kid. A Recess app is built as a template, and each reader gets their own version with their own content, no rebuild needed. The look is always Recess's. What changes is the content.
  • Where the interests come from: your list, Rocky's running notes on each kid, or the kid picking a theme at the start of a chapter.
  • Two ways to start. prepare opens with a worked example, for a kid seeing the skill for the first time. remediate opens by working through a mistake that kid tends to make.
  • Follow-ups. Turn on applet follow-ups for the goal, and a mistake the kid keeps making can trigger an extra practice applet.

The build order

First the map, then one pilot chapter, then the rest in batches. There are three approval points, and nothing goes to Recess until you say yes.

  1. You send the course-map prompt and the first chapter's pages.

  2. Claude drafts a course map here in chat: the skill, game, debate question, and interest hooks for every chapter.

    You approve the map

  3. Claude builds Chapter 1 as a pilot, with the module, the game, and the interest widget, all drafted here with a walkthrough.

    You approve the drafts

  4. Recess shows a preview of exactly what will be created. It's created only after that, and assigned only when you say.

    You approve the preview

  5. Play the pilot yourself first, then with a kid. Tell Claude what dragged, what was too easy, and what they loved.

  6. Claude builds the remaining chapters three at a time, through the same approval points. Once it works, save it as a template to reuse with another kid or another book.
